### 1. Relevance of the Korean Won (USD/KRW) for Korean Semiconductor Returns

The Korean won (USD/KRW) is a first-order macro variable for Korean semiconductor equities (e.g., Samsung Electronics, SK Hynix) for three interconnected reasons: the export-driven nature of their business model, the commodity pricing cycle of their core products, and the behavior of foreign investors.

*   **Export-Driven Earnings Sensitivity:** The Korean economy is fundamentally export-oriented, with semiconductors being its single largest export category. Korean semiconductor firms incur a significant portion of their costs (labor, domestic capital expenditure) in KRW but generate the vast majority of their revenue in USD, as memory chips (DRAM, NAND) are global commodities priced in dollars. Consequently, a depreciation of the won (a higher USD/KRW) directly translates to higher revenues and operating profits when repatriated into KRW, even if USD-denominated average selling prices (ASPs) are flat. This creates a direct, positive mechanical link between KRW weakness and the firms' reported earnings, which in turn influences their equity returns. Research has consistently highlighted the sensitivity of Korean exporters' profitability and stock prices to currency movements (Park, 2012).

*   **Memory Cycle Amplification:** The global memory market is intensely cyclical, driven by supply-demand imbalances that cause large swings in ASPs. The KRW often acts as a pro-cyclical amplifier. During global downturns, demand for electronics and memory chips falls, putting downward pressure on USD-denominated ASPs. Simultaneously, these risk-off periods often see a flight to safety, strengthening the USD and weakening the KRW. While the falling ASPs hurt revenues, the weaker KRW provides a partial cushion on KRW-denominated financials. Conversely, during a global upswing, rising ASPs are often accompanied by a stronger KRW, which dampens some of the upside. The "KRW-weak" regime you referenced likely coincides with the trough or early recovery phase of the memory cycle, where the currency effect provides a powerful tailwind to battered valuations.

*   **Foreign Investor Flows:** Foreign investors are a dominant force in the Korean stock market (KOSPI). Their investment decisions are sensitive to both expected equity returns and expected currency returns. A depreciating KRW can trigger outflows as it erodes the USD-denominated returns for foreign investors. **Choe, Kho, and Stulz (1999)**, in their seminal study on the Asian Financial Crisis, found that foreign investors in Korea exhibited herding behavior, and their selling pressure significantly impacted stock returns, even after controlling for other factors. While their study focused on a crisis period, the core finding—that foreign flows are a powerful driver of returns—remains salient. Therefore, the USD/KRW exchange rate is not just an earnings translator but also a key variable influencing the risk appetite and flow direction of the marginal, price-setting investor in the Korean market.

### 2. Relevant Macro Regime Axes for US Semiconductors

The macro drivers for US semiconductors are fundamentally different from Korea's, originating from the US's position as the center of global capital markets and its role as the issuer of the world's reserve currency. The relevant regime axes are thus tied to domestic financial conditions and global risk pricing.

*   **Credit Spreads (e.g., ICE BofA US High Yield OAS):** High-yield option-adjusted spreads (HY OAS) are a premier indicator of risk appetite and financial conditions in the US economy. Semiconductors are a deeply cyclical, high-beta industry requiring immense capital expenditure. Widening credit spreads signal tightening financial conditions, higher costs of capital, and investor risk aversion. This directly impacts semiconductor firms by making it more expensive to fund R&D and new fabrication plants (fabs). It also signals an expected economic slowdown, which would curb end-demand for electronics. Therefore, a "wide-spread" regime is typically negative for semiconductor equity returns.

*   **10-Year Real Interest Rate (e.g., DFII10):** The real interest rate is arguably the most critical valuation driver for high-growth technology stocks. US semiconductor firms, particularly in the fabless and GPU space (e.g., NVIDIA), are valued on the basis of very high expected growth in cash flows far into the future. This makes them long-duration assets. In asset pricing, the value of a stock is the present value of its future cash flows, discounted by a risk-adjusted rate. The real interest rate is a foundational component of this discount rate.
    *   **Equity Duration:** As established in the literature, stocks with higher growth prospects have longer "equity durations," meaning their valuations are more sensitive to changes in the discount rate. **Dechow, Sloan, and Soliman (2004)** pioneered methods to estimate implied equity duration, showing that growth stocks have durations far exceeding those of value stocks. More recently, **Gormsen and Lazarus (2023)** developed a robust framework for measuring equity duration and confirmed empirically that stocks with the highest expected cash-flow growth (like leading-edge tech) exhibit extreme sensitivity to interest rate movements. A rising real rate (a "high-rate" regime) significantly compresses the present value of these distant cash flows, leading to sharp valuation contractions, and vice versa.

*   **Broad Dollar Index (e.g., DXY):** While less direct than for Korea, the US dollar is still relevant. Most US semiconductor firms are global multinationals. A stronger dollar has a negative translation effect on their overseas earnings and makes their products more expensive for foreign customers, potentially dampening demand. However, this effect is secondary to domestic financial conditions and real rates, which drive valuation multiples more directly.

### 3. AI Capex Reflexivity and Sustainability Risk

The concern around AI capital expenditure (capex) reflexivity posits that the current boom is a self-reinforcing loop that is vulnerable to a sharp reversal. The theory, drawing on Soros's concept of reflexivity, is that massive hyperscaler spending on NVIDIA GPUs drives up NVIDIA's revenue and stock price. NVIDIA's soaring market capitalization and cash generation then enable it to invest in AI startups (e.g., CoreWeave), who in turn use the capital to purchase more NVIDIA GPUs. This creates a "circular" flow of funds that can inflate demand beyond its organic, sustainable level.

Commentary on this risk has emerged primarily from sell-side research and industry experts between 2023-2024:

*   **Sell-Side Commentary:** Analysts have raised concerns about a potential "digestion phase." For instance, **Morgan Stanley's equity analyst Joseph Moore** has frequently discussed the sustainability of AI spending, questioning the timing and magnitude of the return on investment for hyperscalers and the risk of a temporary pause or slowdown in orders once initial infrastructure is built out (verifiable via numerous research notes and public interviews, 2023-2024).
*   **Industry Executive Commentary:** The risk of overbuilding is a perennial concern. **TSMC CEO C.C. Wei** has provided commentary during earnings calls (2023-2024) about the sustainability of AI-related demand, often expressing confidence but acknowledging the need to monitor end-market adoption closely to avoid a supply glut.
*   **"Circular Financing" Risk:** The specific risk of NVIDIA's venture investments fueling its own sales has been highlighted by short-sellers and in investigative financial journalism. While the scale of these investments relative to NVIDIA's total revenue is debated, it adds a layer of reflexivity to the demand story. Sources include reports from outlets like The Information and various financial blogs that have analyzed NVIDIA's venture portfolio.

At present, this remains a risk factor rather than a consensus view, and there is no significant academic literature on this specific 2023-2024 cycle. The bull case rests on a continued explosion in AI inference demand and enterprise adoption, justifying the current capex. The bear case fears an air pocket if this monetization fails to materialize quickly enough.

### 4. Structural Heterogeneity Within US Semiconductors

Pooling all US semiconductor stocks into a single cross-section is problematic because the sector is highly heterogeneous. Different sub-groups have distinct business models, cyclicalities, and macro sensitivities. This dilutes factor signals.

| Sub-Group | Examples | Business Model & Characteristics | Cyclicality & Macro Sensitivity |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Memory** | Micron (MU) | Commodity producer (DRAM/NAND). High fixed costs, capex-intensive. Profitability is a direct function of global ASPs. | **Very High** cyclicality. Sensitive to global electronics demand and supply discipline. Behaves most like Korean peers. |
| **Logic/Analog** | TI (TXN), ADI | Diversified products for industrial, automotive. "Sticky" customer relationships, long product life cycles. High, stable gross margins. | **Low-to-Moderate** cyclicality. More correlated with broad industrial production cycles than consumer electronics. Less volatile. |
| **Fabless (GPU/Logic)** | NVIDIA (NVDA), AMD, Broadcom (AVGO), Qualcomm (QCOM) | Designs chips, outsources manufacturing (to TSMC). High R&D, high gross margins. Success depends on design leadership in secular growth markets (AI, mobile, networking). | **High** cyclicality, but driven by specific tech cycles (e.g., AI, 5G). Extremely sensitive to real rates due to high-growth profile (long duration). |
| **Equipment** | AMAT, LRCX, KLAC | Sells the "picks and shovels" to fabs. Revenue is driven by the capex budgets of TSMC, Samsung, Intel. | **Very High** cyclicality; often leads the chip cycle. Their orders are a key forward-looking indicator for the entire industry. |
| **IDM** | Intel (INTC) | Integrated Device Manufacturer (designs and manufactures). High fixed costs of fabs. Faces direct competition from fabless/foundry model. | **High** cyclicality, compounded by firm-specific execution challenges. A hybrid of sensitivities. |

**Why Pooling Dilutes Signals:** A regime-conditional study on this pooled group would be noisy. For example, in a "high real-rate" regime, long-duration NVDA might underperform dramatically, while lower-duration, more stable TXN might hold up relatively well. Lumping them together would obscure this powerful signal. Similarly, a factor like "Change in DRAM Prices" would be a strong predictor for MU's returns but almost irrelevant for ADI. Segregating the universe into these more homogeneous sub-groups is essential for isolating clear factor relationships.

### 5. Momentum vs. Reversal in Semiconductors

The question of whether momentum in semiconductors is persistent (trending) or prone to reversal is best answered by considering the heterogeneity described above.

*   **Korean/Memory-like Semis:** The finding of 12-month momentum *reversal* in Korean semis aligns perfectly with their commodity-cyclical nature. In such sectors, strong positive momentum often occurs as prices rise toward a cyclical peak. At the peak, fundamentals are excellent, but the next marginal move is inevitably down. This mean-reversion dynamic causes momentum strategies to fail spectacularly at turning points. Research by **Daniel and Moskowitz (2016)** shows that momentum strategies are prone to "crashes," which often occur when bear markets end and previously beaten-down, high-beta, value-like stocks (a good description of memory stocks at a cycle trough) rally sharply.

*   **US Semiconductors:** The US landscape is mixed.
    *   For **Micron (MU)**, one would expect a pattern similar to its Korean peers: strong mean-reversion and fragile momentum signals around cycle peaks and troughs.
    *   For fabless growth leaders like **NVIDIA**, the story has been one of powerful, *persistent* trend momentum, driven by the secular AI theme. This trend has lasted much longer than a typical memory cycle. However, the extreme valuation and concentration risk imply that if the trend breaks, the reversal could be just as violent as in commodity-like names.
    *   General evidence on industry momentum, starting with **Moskowitz and Grinblatt (1999)**, suggests that industry-level trends can be persistent. For a time, "semiconductors" as a group benefited from this. However, the increasing dominance of the AI narrative makes it a specific thematic momentum rather than a broad cyclical one.

In summary, momentum in commodity-like semiconductor segments is likely to exhibit strong reversal tendencies, while momentum in segments driven by secular technological shifts (like AI) may be more persistent but are exposed to a severe crash risk if the underlying narrative falters.

### 6. Why Korean Indicators Cannot Be Directly Transplanted to the US

Transplanting the "KRW-weak" regime axis directly to a US study would be methodologically unsound due to fundamental structural differences:

1.  **Dominant Currency Role:** For Korea, the KRW is a proxy for the country's export competitiveness and the profitability of its largest firms. For the US, the USD is the world's reserve currency; its fluctuations are driven by global risk appetite, interest rate differentials, and capital flows on a global scale, not just US trade competitiveness. A "strong dollar" regime has a far more complex and less direct impact on US firms compared to the "weak won" effect on Korean exporters.
2.  **Investor Base and Flows:** The Korean market is heavily influenced by foreign investors whose returns are directly impacted by KRW/USD, as discussed by **Choe, Kho, and Stulz (1999)**. The US equity market is far deeper and dominated by domestic investors, for whom the dollar's value is not a direct source of return volatility.
3.  **Industrial and Index Structure:** The KOSPI is highly concentrated in a few large, export-oriented industrial conglomerates (chaebol) like Samsung. The US market is much broader, and while the SOX index is specific to semis, its internal composition is diverse (as per Q4). The recent extreme concentration of the SOXX and SMH ETFs in NVIDIA (often >20%) means that the index's behavior is now disproportionately the behavior of one long-duration, AI-driven stock, a structure vastly different from the Samsung/Hynix duopoly in the memory-focused Korean market.
4.  **Macro Linkages:** Korean semi returns are tied to the global manufacturing and electronics cycle, with the KRW acting as a key transmission mechanism. US semi returns, especially for market leaders, are increasingly tied to US financial conditions (real rates, credit spreads) and secular themes like AI, which are valuation- and discount-rate-driven phenomena.

Therefore, a US-centric study requires a regime axis grounded in the drivers of US financial conditions and valuation, such as real rates and credit spreads, rather than a currency-based export model.
